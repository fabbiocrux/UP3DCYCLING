Exploring the applicability of the Open Source Technology Readiness Levels (OSTRL) framework to circular economy practices in LAUDS Factories

Opening Frame

In recent years the advancement of digital communities make possible the development of an open design approach, becoming source for product innovation instead of conventional and proprietary product development (Shi and Jiang 2025; Bakırlıoğlu and Kohtala 2019). Aiming at a ‘design global / manufacturing local’ (Kostakis et al. 2018) seems a proto-industrialization (Sabel and Zeitlin 1985) transition that is taking place in urban settlements that could a major impact in the next short future. The Open Source Appropriate Technology (OSAT)(Pearce et al. 2010) and peer-to-peer (P2P)(Kostakis 2013) approaches have been seen potential drivers to propose an alternative globalisation manufacturing paradigm (Heikkinen 2020). Indeed, open-design can be defined as ’the state of a design project where both the process and the sources of its outputs are accessible and (re)usable, by anyone and for any purpose’(Boisseau et al. 2018). In that sense, open design make it possible the development of small-scale, economically affordable, decentralised, energy-efficient, environmentally sound technologies that could easily utilized by local communities to meet their needs (pearce2012b?; Pacheco et al. 2025). This approach have been valuable for software development because free and open source software (FOSS) providing: i) diversification and open innovation (colombo2014?), ii) cumulative innovation (boudreau2016?), iii) development efficiency (Hienerth et al. 2014), iv) organizational innovation (Alexy et al. 2013), v) higher technical quality of code (soderberg2015?), vi) encourages creativity (martinez2015?) and vii) perhaps most importantly, it avoids redundant work (Årdal and Røttingen 2016). Likewise, scientific equipement to reduce the cost with equal of quality (pearce2014k?; Pearce 2016), and having implication in national level (Pearce 2022). By relying on local resources, materials, and competencies, these approaches often challenge conventional product development models, including product functions and consumption patterns. An open source technodiversity could be a strong breakthrough contribution to sustainability and CE than traditional manufacturing paradigms to foster the creation of urban closed-loop supply chains inside urban settlements.

According the Open Source Hardware Association, the development of open hardware defines :

Hardware whose design is made publicly available so that anyone can study, modify, distribute, make, and sell the design or hardware based on that design. The hardware’s source, the design from which it is made, is available in the preferred format for making modifications to it. Ideally, open source hardware uses readily-available components and materials, standard processes, open infrastructure, unrestricted content, and open-source design tools to maximize the ability of individuals to make and use hardware. Open source hardware gives people the freedom to control their technology while sharing knowledge and encouraging commerce through the open exchange of designs

However, one major question remains. As OS hardware matures from a largely hobbyist-driven activity to a professional form of technological development:

  1. How can be mapped the maturity level of the artifacts that are issue from an open desing approach?.
  2. What are the observable traces that make explain the evolution of the design towards a complete openess ?, and
  3. In what extent open-source (OS) technologies could be synergic support of sustainable and circular economy (CE) practices, especially in urban manufacturing contexts.

While OS hardware has been shown to offer over 90% cost savings for research equipment and fosters increased innovation within national economies [heikkinen2020], the absence of robust methodologies to assess the real-world implementation of OS technologies in CE contexts remains challenging, especially considering readiness, transferability, and maturity. Although several evaluation frameworks exist, e.g., material circularity index, most focus on environmental or system-level perspectives, often limiting practitioners (makers, tier-places) and researchers working on real-world OS technologies.

Key concepts & Framework

Urban manufacturing (Herrmann et al. 2020; Juraschek 2022; Herrmann et al. 2019) is increasingly seen as a pathway to re-localize production through cleaner and more sustainable processes. Customised products to be produced in regions close to the consumer that create a virtuous cycle based on design-produce-use-repair-recycle loops may alternative paths resource scarcity, circularity (Sajadieh et al. 2022). This decentralized approach contrast with the current mass manufacturing paradigm are often globalized value chains products. The mass production system, in the lens of a deep transition framework, is the fruit of a complex co-evolution of single unit productions and interconnected systems (Kanger et al. 2022). However, several forms of environmental degradation have been intensified without fully assuring the social foundation’s minimum standards (e.g. healthcare, energy, water etc) (Raworth 2017).

To support their continuous adoption, the establishment the OS Technology Readiness Levels (OSTRL) framework is aims to establish a a technological blueprint in the development. OSTRL was originally developed to translate readiness into OS contexts, facilitating the assessment and development of openness in OS technologies. This project aims to refine the OSTRL framework and extend its use by testing its applicability within CE contexts beyond DRAM ecosystems. Following a practice-oriented approach, the proposal involves potential target users in evaluating real-world OS projects using OSTRLs and collecting structured feedback on their clarity, usability, and limitations. The OSTRLs are refined accordingly, and complementary uses with CE-focused assessment frameworks are evaluated for real-world adoption.

LUADS in Practice

  • The starting activity is a structured workshop organized within the Horizon 2020 LAUDS project (https://lauds.eu/) meeting hosted at ERPI (Fall 2026). Researchers, project partners, and practitioners will apply OSTRLs to selected CE-related case studies from LAUDS through guided activities. The feedback collected during the workshop, together with further feedback at ERPI during the visiting period, will be used to refine the OSTRLs, improving their clarity and applicability. The expected outcomes include: an updated, tested version of the OSTRLs; refined use guidelines and documentation; and additional case studies as examples of OSTRL use. These elements will be included in a peer-reviewed publication and OS repositories for open dissemination and future follow-up.
